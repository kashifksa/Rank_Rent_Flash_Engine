TITLE : Is air duct cleaning a scam? Truth from HVAC technicians

[1] The whole-house duct cleaning for ninety-nine dollars you see plastered across community Facebook groups and mailer coupons is ninety-nine percent a bait-and-switch operation. Legitimate air duct cleaning requires a heavy commercial truck-mounted vacuum or a high-powered HEPA negative air machine that pulls thousands of cubic feet of air per minute. That equipment alone costs tens of thousands of dollars. No legitimate crew can roll up with two technicians and clean fifteen vents for under a hundred bucks.

[2] What actually happens on those low-cost specials is the blow-and-go routine. They bring in a shop vac or a small handheld unit, hook it up to one register, and run a rotary brush for ten minutes. Then the technician comes upstairs holding a piece of black insulation or dirty swab and claims your entire trunk line is covered in toxic black mold. Suddenly that ninety-nine dollar visit turns into an aggressive sales pitch for fourteen hundred dollars of chemical sanitizing and UV light installations.

[3] According to EPA guidelines, routine duct cleaning is not something you need to do every single year. You should clean your ducts if there is visible mold growth on hard surface ducts, if you have rodent or insect droppings inside the supply trunks, or after major construction or remodeling where drywall dust and sawdust were pulled into the return air vents. If you keep high-quality MERV eleven filters in place and change them every three months, the supply ducts stay remarkably clean.

[4] If you are hiring someone to clean your ducts, look for NADCA certification. National Air Duct Cleaners Association members must follow standard ACR two thousand thirteen. They put the entire duct system under negative pressure before agitating the debris from the furthest vent back to the vacuum. Anything less than source removal under negative pressure is just stirring dust around your house.

[5] A key tell of an amateur operation is failing to seal off the remaining vents. When you pull negative pressure on the plenum, every supply register in the house needs to be covered with magnetic or foam seals. Otherwise you lose air velocity at the far branch runs, and the dust simply settles in the middle of your ductwork instead of reaching the collection bag.

TITLE : Recommended inspection and cleaning frequency for residential HVAC

[1] In general residential environments, having your ductwork professionally inspected every three to five years is the gold standard recommended by NADCA. In homes with shedding pets, active smokers, or residents suffering from respiratory conditions like asthma, having them cleaned every two to three years provides measurable relief.

[2] You do not need cleaning if an inspection reveals only a thin dusting on the walls of the supply lines. Air duct metal naturally develops a microscopic surface film that holds fine dust without releasing it into the airflow. Cleaning is required when visible debris rolls into clumps, registers show black streaks, or when airflow through distant rooms drops significantly.

[3] After any home renovation involving tile cutting, drywall sanding, hardwood refinishing, or kitchen remodeling, duct cleaning is mandatory. Silica dust from drywall mud and crystalline particulates from flooring pass straight through standard fiberglass filters and settle deep inside the furnace heat exchanger.

[4] Always request that the contractor reseal any access openings in the main trunk with metal patch plates fastened by sheet metal screws and approved aluminum foil tape. Never accept cardboard plugs or duct tape covers, which dry out, peel off, and leak conditioned air directly into your crawlspace or basement.
